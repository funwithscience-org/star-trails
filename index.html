Two Disks, One Sky

A flat-earth demo gets counter-rotating star trails by projecting two spinning light disks onto one dome and filming from outside. The trick is real, but it is a camera trick. The question that matters is what two disks look like from underneath, and whether they can ever produce the straight trails we photograph at the equator.

On screen, field A turns — field B turns — camera outside the dome disks actually spin the same way
What to notice

Both disks spin the same way. From outside you look at field A's back (through the dome shell) and at field B's front (the inside surface, seen across the dome). A rotation seen from behind is mirrored, so A appears to counter-rotate against B. Slide the camera inside the dome, where any observer on a flat plane would actually be, and both fields turn the same way.

Their side of it

A flat earther can skip the camera trick entirely and simply spin the second disk the other way. Press Reverse disk B to grant that. It fixes the direction, so counter-rotation on its own is not the problem. Tab 2 shows what is.

What to notice

This is the whole sky seen from underneath, zenith in the centre, horizon at the rim (north up, east left, as on a star chart). Grow the radius and the two fields have to overlap. In the overlap you get two sets of trails crossing through each other, going opposite ways or the same way with opposite curvature. No photograph has ever shown that.

The real equatorial sky shows something two disks can never make: straight trails running east–west through the zenith, bending one way toward the north horizon and the other way toward the south. Press Globe sky to see what that takes: the second pole has to be the exact antipode of the first, which puts it below a flat earth, not on a dome above it.

Their side of it

"Keep the disks small, so they never overlap." Then each disk covers at most half the angular distance between the poles, and everything between them is a starless band. Yet Orion, Sirius and the whole zodiac are seen from both hemispheres on the same night. The stars they share are precisely the ones that must sit in both disks at once.

What to notice

A sphere drawn around you is a map of directions. Any radius will do, because a direction has no distance in it; that is why a planetarium works and why astronomers have drawn the celestial sphere for two thousand years. So one observer's personal sphere reproduces everything they see. Build a second one 954 km away and the same star is now marked in two places, because each sphere holds a copy of the direction, not the star. Expand the spheres: the two copies never meet. The rays do, at infinity, and that is where the star is as far as any pair of observers can tell.

The one thing the two spheres do not share is "up". Chicago's zenith and Washington's differ by 8.6°, and Polaris stands 3° higher over Chicago for exactly that reason: parallel direction, tilted horizons. On a flat plane every "up" is parallel, and both cities would put Polaris at the same altitude.

A sphere turning about an axis through its centre has two fixed points, not one. In each personal sphere the axis enters at the north celestial pole and exits at the south celestial pole, 40° below the southern horizon for these two cities and above it for anyone south of the equator. That second point is not optical. It is what rotating a sphere means. A single disk over the north has one fixed point and cannot produce it.

Their side of it

"The second pole is an optical effect of the observer's celestial sphere; the physical rotation is one disk around Polaris." Grant the sphere, it is a legitimate tool. Then switch on Stars ride on the sphere, which takes the claim literally: the stars are physically on Chicago's sphere. Washington now sees every star in a measurably different direction, and the readout gives the number. Real star positions agree between any two cities on Earth to a fraction of an arcsecond, which puts any physical sphere out past the orbit of Saturn on this baseline alone, and out to light-years once the Earth's orbit is used as the baseline.

What the videos show

Two videos, one device. A clear acrylic dome over a printed flat-earth map, a two-way mirror disk held by magnets inside it (near the top on 13 December, lower on 12 December), and a lens-type star projector outside the dome on a box, below the dome's base, pointing almost straight up (13 Dec, 7:20); on 12 December it stands further back, "between the telescopes" (0:39). Its cone paints one star field on the ceiling and never touches the dome. Everything that appears "on" the dome is that ceiling seen in a mirror: in the acrylic's convex outer surface and in the two-way mirror. (The far inner wall cannot do it: its normal faces inward, so ceiling light reaching it goes to the map, and the mirror is too small to feed it a second bounce that could leave the dome; the model solves for both and finds nothing.) At 9:17 on 13 December he films straight down and calls the dome "the single star field stretched across the whole round": his own hands and phone are reflected in it beside the stars, and the stars are missing where his body blocks the ceiling. A reflection of the ceiling behaves exactly that way; light painted on the glass would not.

Every reflection is a copy of the one field, reversed, seen from the opposite side. The ceiling is seen from below; its images in the dome are seen from above. So the ceiling and any of its reflections turn opposite ways on screen, and two reflections turn the same way as each other. In his close-ups the two reflections sit one above the other, the cap image over the mirror image, and where they meet at the mirror's rim the lower edge of one sweeps one way while the upper edge of the other sweeps the other, like two gears turning the same way where their teeth touch. That seam is what he calls the equator, and the shear across it is what he calls arc and anti-arc. His 6:44 "Equatorial Star Trails" still is a hard zoom on that seam turned through 90°, and the trails in it are still nested arcs. The readout labels each copy "direct" or "reflected", and the reflections move with the camera because reflections do, which is his "it follows no matter where you go".

The storyboard above the picture walks his shots in order, each with a short clip from the video beside the model set to the same view. The seam arrows are measured: the model moves the projector a few degrees, finds where the cap reflection and the mirror reflection went on screen just above and just below the mirror's top edge, and draws the result. Which way each turns depends on where the camera is, so the readout says what it sees rather than asserting a sense; what never changes is that the two reflections agree with each other and disagree with the ceiling. The gauge on the left tracks the camera's height. Above the mirror plane, where every one of his dual-spiral shots is taken, the mirror shows the ceiling; below it, as at 6:58 on 13 December, the mirror shows the map and the room and the second spiral is gone. His southern sky exists only for a camera floating above the mirror looking down. The Ghost toggle draws what the mirror would give a fully starred dome: the image of the part of the dome above the mirror, hanging below it. With the mirror this high that is a shallow slice, not a southern hemisphere; a full lower half needs the mirror at the base, and either way the one place an observer could stand to see both halves is on the mirror itself, looking down through the floor at a Dipper whose handle bends the wrong way.

The sliders also let the projector's cone reach the dome, in which case beams light the glass directly and bounce off the mirror; those copies are drawn in white and the readout reports them. On his settings they are empty. Switch the mirror off and its image vanishes; switch the outer-surface reflection off and the dome goes dark. What remains is a ceiling and a projector.

The projector carries a Dipper-shaped asterism so the mirrored copy can be checked by eye: the handle bends the other way. Orion photographed from Australia is Orion turned upside down, belt and sword in the same order as from Europe: a rotation, not a reflection. The southern sky also contains stars with no northern counterpart at all. A mirror cannot produce either.

Where tab 1 lives in his rig

Tab 1's trick is one rotation seen from two sides. In his rig the two sides are the ceiling itself, which you see from below, and any reflection of it, which you see from above. Press 6 · Under the mirror: the ceiling field is in the top of the frame in blue, its reflection in the dome's cap is below it in pink, and the readout gives them opposite senses, "seen from below" against "reflected, so reversed". That pair is the "two counter-rotating disks": same field, same spin, one of them in a mirror. His 2:52 close-up has a soft near layer and a sharp far layer. The near one is the cap's convex reflection (its virtual image sits just behind the glass, so a phone focused deeper blurs it). The far one is one of two things, and the footage decides which: the ceiling seen in the flat mirror, bounded by the mirror's ellipse with the telescopes reflected in it, which turns the same way as the cap reflection and only sweeps against it at the seam, the gears; or the ceiling itself seen straight through the glass past the far side of the dome, bounded by the dome's outline, which turns the opposite way everywhere, tab 1 exactly. The model shows both, and the readout names the sense of each; what it will not do is call either one a second star field. Tab 2's point survives either way: two separate spiral centres in one sky, whichever way they turn, leave a seam of straight, oppositely bowing or colliding trails between them, and that is exactly what his "Equatorial Star Trails" crop shows. The real equator has no seam because the real sky has one axis, and its two poles are the two ends of it.

Their side of it

"The two-way mirror stands in for the superfluid ceiling; the real sky reflects the luminaries the same way." Grant it, and follow the geometry. A mirror lying flat above you reflects what is below it. For it to show you reflected stars, the stars must hang between you and the mirror, and then every star has a twin directly above it in the sky, the real one and its reflection, both visible at once. No star has a twin. Tilt the mirror instead, to throw the north's reflection into the south, and the reflection's position depends on where you stand, which he demonstrates himself: "it follows no matter where you go." The south celestial pole does not follow anyone. It sits at a fixed bearing, at an altitude set only by latitude, for every observer at once.
